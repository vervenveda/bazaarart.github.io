KHAEMENES ACADEMY · ART 101 · UNIT 12 · WEEK 33

Community Brief and Collaborative Design

Art, Community and Curatorial Practice · Original visual inquiry and studio instruction

Essential question

How can a community art proposal respond to actual needs, consent and constraints without claiming to speak for everyone?

This course supports teenagers and adults and includes equipment-free alternatives. Studio evidence and quiz scores are stored in this browser only; they are not authenticated grades, identity or automatic course credit.

Core vocabulary and reasoning

Creative brief

A written agreement or plan describing a design problem, audience, outputs and limits.

Stakeholder

A person or group affected by a proposed artwork, venue or design.

Participation

Optional informed involvement in research, feedback or making decisions.

Constraints

Real limits such as time, space, budget, safety, materials and accessibility.

Co-design

An approach that includes relevant people in shaping decisions rather than only receiving a finished product.

Prototype

A low-cost preliminary model used to make questions testable.

Feedback synthesis

A summary that differentiates recurring concerns, conflicting opinions and design responses.

Original worked example

A fictional community library requests a visual welcome wall. The first draft is decorative but blocks directions to accessible restrooms and assumes every visitor reads English.

  1. Write the brief: purpose is welcome and orientation; audiences include new visitors and people with different reading abilities; constraints include a 2×3 meter wall, no blocked signage and a modest fictional budget.
  2. Develop two paper prototype alternatives, one with clear arrows and one focused on decorative silhouettes. Retain a readable text route independent of color.
  3. Ask optional viewers to locate the exit and library desk; record feedback anonymously without storing personal contact information.
  4. Revise a concept to preserve public circulation, clear signs and a role for community-submitted motifs only with consent and attribution. Document whose suggestions were used.

Five-stage studio investigation

01 · Observe and document

Observe: Inspect a permitted community space or use the fictional library scenario. Record audience tasks, sightlines, safety, traffic flow and signs that must remain visible.

02 · Research and attribute

Research: Study two attributed public or community art examples. Look for documentation of consultation, authorship, permissions and maintenance; avoid assuming all residents endorse a single aesthetic.

03 · Experiment and construct

Experiment: Draft a one-page creative brief and two inexpensive paper models. Define the specific feedback question and accessibility risks before seeking voluntary responses.

04 · Create, critique and revise

Create and revise: Collect anonymous optional responses or use an openly labeled simulated critique. Summarize supportive and conflicting perspectives, then revise the proposal with a documented decision log.

05 · Reflect, archive and seek review

Reflect and archive: Provide the brief, two prototypes, realistic budget categories, a rights/permissions checklist and a statement that the proposal does not claim community-wide endorsement.

Independent studio assignment

Create an actionable community-art proposal with a defined audience, two co-design prototypes, a six-category budget, risk and maintenance notes, anonymous optional feedback, and a revised proposal with credit policy.

No-special-equipment alternative

Use an invented community scenario and role-play feedback without collecting any real personal data. Paper cutouts or a cardboard site model are sufficient.

Privacy, safety and rights

Do not collect identifiable student details, publish private participant input or represent a class poll as public consensus. Adult authorization is necessary for actual installations.

Research and attribution

Explore these institutional starting points. Choose specific attributed works yourself and record creator, title, date if known, accession or catalog page, rights details, and what remains uncertain.

  • Cooper Hewitt · Smithsonian Design Museum. Verify creator, title, date, page and reuse rights for any specific artwork selected.
  • Smithsonian · Accessible exhibition guidance. Verify creator, title, date, page and reuse rights for any specific artwork selected.

Collections and archives contain materials with varied rights. Public accessibility does not automatically establish permission to republish.

Formative practice · Five randomized questions

Five of fifteen authored items are drawn at random and answer choices are shuffled. Answer all questions before submitting; answers lock after submission. Four correct is the 80% practice target, not an Academy authorization or mastery certificate.

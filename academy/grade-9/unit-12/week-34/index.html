KHAEMENES ACADEMY · ART 101 · UNIT 12 · WEEK 34

Curating an Exhibition

Art, Community and Curatorial Practice · Original visual inquiry and studio instruction

Essential question

How can an exhibition sequence, labels and spatial plan make art understandable and accessible without overriding the artists' voices?

This course supports teenagers and adults and includes equipment-free alternatives. Studio evidence and quiz scores are stored in this browser only; they are not authenticated grades, identity or automatic course credit.

Core vocabulary and reasoning

Curatorial thesis

A concise claim or question connecting selected works in a public presentation.

Wall label

A short text identifying a work and providing relevant context.

Object checklist

An inventory of title, artist, dimensions, medium, status and display needs.

Sightline

A viewing path or line of vision through exhibition space.

Sequence

The intended order or relationship among works as visitors move.

Access pathway

A route allowing visitors with different mobility and sensory needs to participate.

Interpretive text

Material helping viewers understand context while distinguishing documented facts and opinion.

Original worked example

A small exhibition of six student works called 'Everyday Materials' initially places a fragile clay sculpture near the entry door and hangs long labels beside a narrow walkway.

  1. Write a one-sentence curatorial question: 'How do artists transform ordinary tools into personal symbols?' Select six works with permission and list their media, dates, dimensions and display constraints.
  2. Lay out a scaled gallery floor plan with a clear entrance, accessible movement path and a protected location for fragile sculpture. Use paper rectangles to test alternative placements.
  3. Write one 40-word object label and one 120-word introductory wall text. Check readability, respectful artist attribution and that no claims invent personal stories.
  4. Test the route with a paper visitor avatar or optional audience trial, revise a sightline conflict and prepare a simple digital or large-print text alternative.

Five-stage studio investigation

01 · Observe and document

Observe: Analyze a museum floor plan and wall text examples, noting grouping, label readability, visibility, rest points and where a visitor can choose different routes.

02 · Research and attribute

Research: Consult published exhibition accessibility resources and two credited collection records. Identify how curator interpretation relates to the actual artist or collection information.

03 · Experiment and construct

Experiment: Select six permitted works (original, fictional mock objects or cited reproductions). Produce two floor plans and test how sequence changes their interpreted relationship.

04 · Create, critique and revise

Create and revise: Build a paper or digital gallery with a clear thesis, 40-word labels, introductory text, an object checklist and a route that does not block access. Record one practical revision.

05 · Reflect, archive and seek review

Reflect and archive: Publish a scaled floor plan, labels, complete attribution and permissions, accessible alternatives and a short explanation of the curatorial choices and limits.

Independent studio assignment

Curate a six-object exhibition with title, one-sentence thesis, six accurate labels, a source/permissions checklist, accessible floor plan, two sequence options, a 120-word wall introduction, and a documented critique/revision.

No-special-equipment alternative

Use a tabletop miniature with printed artwork thumbnails or original sketches; a physical gallery or permission to occupy public space is not required.

Privacy, safety and rights

Never assume permission to publicly publish a classmate's artwork. Protect private student names and support large-print and descriptive alternatives.

Research and attribution

Explore these institutional starting points. Choose specific attributed works yourself and record creator, title, date if known, accession or catalog page, rights details, and what remains uncertain.

  • Smithsonian · Accessible exhibition guidance. Verify creator, title, date, page and reuse rights for any specific artwork selected.
  • Cooper Hewitt · Accessibility resources. Verify creator, title, date, page and reuse rights for any specific artwork selected.

Collections and archives contain materials with varied rights. Public accessibility does not automatically establish permission to republish.

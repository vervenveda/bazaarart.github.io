KHAEMENES ACADEMY · ART 101 · UNIT 04

Week 08 · Focal Point and Visual Hierarchy

Observe · Compose · Revise · Explain

Essential question

How can an artist guide attention through an image without telling viewers where to look?

Learning goals: distinguish focal point from visual hierarchy; identify placement, scale, contrast and directional cues; test multiple compositions; produce and evaluate one original artwork.

Four tools for directing attention

Placement

Position influences how viewers scan an image. The center is powerful but predictable; off-center subjects can create tension or invite movement. The rule of thirds is a planning aid, not a universal law.

Scale

Large forms often attract attention, but size alone does not guarantee importance. Compare the subject with neighboring shapes and the available negative space.

Contrast

A sharp difference in light and dark, saturation, edge clarity, or texture can isolate a focal subject. Contrast must be considered relative to its surroundings.

Directional cues

Edges, gestures, sightlines, repetition, and overlapping shapes can guide a viewer toward or away from the intended subject. These cues should support rather than compete with the focal point.

Original worked visual · Competing attention

Study the visual hierarchy in each arrangement. Which image communicates one clear priority? Which creates competing centers of attention? Describe why rather than relying on one fixed rule.

Studio learning sequence · Composition with intention

Work through the five stages in order. Expand one stage at a time; preserve sketches and notes as evidence of artistic decision-making.

1 · Observe and diagnose visual hierarchy

Choose a documented artwork or a still life you arrange yourself. Before reading its title, identify the first, second, and third areas that attract your attention. Record at least three visible reasons: relative value, scale, edge clarity, saturation, placement, or directional lines. Separate observation (“the brightest shape is near the upper right”) from interpretation (“the shape feels hopeful”).

2 · Worked demonstration: one subject, three priorities

Imagine a small red cup, a tall pale vase, and a dark folded cloth. In draft A, enlarge the cup but keep the vase brightest. In draft B, reduce the cup and place it against the darkest cloth. In draft C, align the cloth’s folds toward the cup while softening the vase edges. Predict the focal point of each draft; then test by showing the thumbnails briefly to someone else. Contrast and directional cues may outweigh raw size.

Measure: describe the brightest-darkest adjacency, the subject’s approximate fraction of the frame, and how much negative space remains. A rule-of-thirds grid can help you compare placement, but it cannot prove quality.

3 · Research and contextualize

Compare two artworks from distinct artists or periods using reliable museum, artist, or collection records. Capture creator, title, approximate date, medium, source URL and access date. Explain how each composition directs attention with observable evidence. Avoid claiming that a historical artist consciously followed a modern compositional rule unless a source documents it.

4 · Make, experiment and revise

Create three thumbnails of one original subject, each with a different hierarchy. Keep the subject recognizable while varying placement, scale, and value contrast. Use the interactive Composition Laboratory to test a hypothesis, then translate the strongest study into a physical or digital artwork. Make a deliberate revision after comparing it in grayscale and at thumbnail size. Record what changed and why.

5 · Critique and archive evidence

Ask a reviewer to point to the first area they notice without seeing your intended focal point. Compare that response with your plan. Save three thumbnails, one revised final, lab settings or observations, source citations, and a 120–200 word reflection describing one successful choice, one limitation and one next experiment.

Interactive Composition Laboratory

Move and resize the focal form, adjust its contrast, and compare it with secondary shapes. Use the grid only as a compositional guide.

Limit: This simplified laboratory illustrates relationships between placement, scale, and value contrast; it does not calculate where a human viewer will actually look.

Week 08 studio assignment · One composition, three drafts

  1. Choose an original subject, such as an object, portrait study, garden scene, architectural feature, or abstract arrangement.
  2. Draw three thumbnail compositions: centered emphasis, off-center emphasis, and intentionally competing emphasis. Annotate scale, negative space, value, and directional cues.
  3. Use the laboratory to test an alternative arrangement. Record which change most improved visual clarity and why.
  4. Develop one finished original artwork in a physical or digital medium, using a purposeful focal point and supporting visual hierarchy.
  5. Archive the three thumbnails, finished artwork, date, medium, and 150–250-word artist statement in the Week 01 portfolio.

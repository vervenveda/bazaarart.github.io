KHAEMENES ACADEMY · ART 101 · UNIT 10 · WEEK 27

Sequence, Documentary Ethics and Context

Photography and Visual Evidence · Original visual inquiry and studio instruction

Essential question

How can the order and captions of images shape a documentary account without misleading its audience?

This course supports teenagers and adults and includes equipment-free alternatives. Studio evidence and quiz scores are stored in this browser only; they are not authenticated grades, identity or automatic course credit.

Core vocabulary and reasoning

Sequence

An intentionally ordered set of images that gives relationships and pacing to a visual narrative.

Documentary context

Relevant information about when, where, by whom and under what circumstances an image was made.

Caption

A short factual description of what a viewer can see, distinguished from interpretation and speculation.

Staging

Directing or rearranging what is photographed; disclosure is essential when an image is presented as documentary.

Consent

Permission appropriate to the subject, audience, use and circumstances; a public location does not automatically remove privacy duties.

Provenance

A traceable record of the origin and changes of an image or source.

Contact sheet

A visual overview of images used to choose sequence and spot omissions or repetitions.

Original worked example

A six-frame visual diary shows an ordinary notebook moving from closed to used. The same frames can imply a misleading story if one omits the preparation photograph.

  1. Frame 1: wide view shows a blank desk and notebook; caption states the observable setting without identifying an address.
  2. Frames 2–4: a hand places a pencil, opens the notebook and draws a line. State that the sequence was arranged for this exercise rather than witnessed as an unplanned event.
  3. Frames 5–6: include the completed sketch and a return to the whole desk. Compare the truthful caption 'A staged demonstration of a drawing session' with the unsupported claim 'An artist worked here for hours'.
  4. Retain the uncropped originals, a six-cell contact sheet, a dated caption ledger and a sentence describing what happens outside the frame.

Five-stage studio investigation

01 · Observe and document

Observe: Study six images from a permitted, preferably non-identifying situation. For each, list visible people or objects, implied action and facts that remain unknown; do not infer intent from an expression.

02 · Research and attribute

Research: Select two attributed visual documents from a trusted archive such as the Library of Congress. Record creator, approximate date, page URL, rights statement and contextual description; compare the archive metadata with your own observations.

03 · Experiment and construct

Experiment: Photograph or draw six frames showing a safe household process. Make two sequences from the same six frames—one chronological and one thematic—and note how pacing and meaning change without inventing facts.

04 · Create, critique and revise

Create and revise: Write a factual caption for each frame, identify any deliberate staging, and ask a viewer to explain what they think happened. Revise at least two captions or placements to eliminate unsupported conclusions.

05 · Reflect, archive and seek review

Reflect and archive: Produce the sequence and source ledger, explain how consent was obtained or why no identifiable people appear, and state two limits of the visual evidence. Export the lesson record separately from the optional images.

Independent studio assignment

Create a six-frame documentary sequence of a permitted everyday process; submit a contact sheet, two distinct orders, six contextual captions, a provenance/consent ledger, two corrected captions and a 150-word statement on image limitations.

No-special-equipment alternative

Use six hand-drawn viewfinder scenes instead of photographs. Work with objects, not identifiable people; mark each frame as an observed drawing or staged recreation.

Privacy, safety and rights

Avoid geolocation, recognizable children, faces and identifying documents. No image may be called candid if it was staged; record licensing for any reference image.

Research and attribution

Explore these institutional starting points. Choose specific attributed works yourself and record creator, title, date if known, accession or catalog page, rights details, and what remains uncertain.

  • Library of Congress · Free to Use and Reuse. Verify creator, title, date, page and reuse rights for any specific artwork selected.
  • Library of Congress · Rights guidance. Verify creator, title, date, page and reuse rights for any specific artwork selected.

Collections and archives contain materials with varied rights. Public accessibility does not automatically establish permission to republish.

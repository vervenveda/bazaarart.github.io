KHAEMENES ACADEMY · ART 101 · UNIT 10

Week 27 · Sequence, Documentary Ethics and Context

Unit 10 · Printmaking and Reproducible Images · Original studio instruction

Essential question

How can six images communicate a sequence responsibly without implying events, causes, identities or consent that the evidence cannot establish?

Art 101 is proficiency-based for teen and adult learners. A camera is optional: six original observation drawings satisfy the studio requirements. Use only your own objects or consented, non-sensitive settings. Local evidence is not an authenticated gradebook.

Core vocabulary and reasoning

Sequence and narrative inference

Image order creates expectations about time, cause and consequence. Adjacent photographs may appear to tell a continuous story even when they were taken at different times. A sequence is an editorial choice, not automatic proof of chronology.

Documentary context and captions

Captions should separate visible observation from source-supported context and interpretation. Record date, place only when safe, whether the scene was arranged, and relevant omissions. Never invent quotations or events.

Consent, staging and representation

Permission to take a picture does not always mean permission to publish it. Avoid identifiable bystanders and private records. Disclose staged scenes, substantive edits and reenactments so viewers can assess evidentiary limits.

Original worked demonstration · A six-frame pencil journey

Arrange a pencil, notebook and closed box on a table. Record six frames: (1) objects at rest; (2) hand-free close-up of pencil; (3) pencil beside notebook; (4) notebook opened; (5) pencil placed on page; (6) completed still life. Photograph or draw the stages yourself. Do not claim that the images prove who moved the objects or how long the process took. If the sequence is staged, label it as a staged demonstration.

Worked comparison: Arrange the same six frames in two orders, A (1–6) and B (6–1). Ask a viewer what they think happened. Note how their interpretation changes even though the image content does not. A viewer's inference is evidence of interpretation, not evidence that the depicted events occurred in that order.

Five-stage studio lesson

01 · Observe and classify

Examine two short visual sequences from appropriately attributed sources. For each, distinguish visible details, captions, chronology claims, and unknown information. Identify at least two places where editing or omission could change meaning.

02 · Research and attribute

Investigate two documentary photography or visual-reporting examples with creator, title or description, date where known, source and publication context. Compare stated ethical practices, such as consent, staging disclosure and caption correction. Do not invent an ethical policy for a source that does not state one.

03 · Construct six original frames

Create six original photographs or drawings of a safe, controlled still life or non-identifying process. Label each with frame number, observed content, whether staged, capture method and any edits. Produce a contact sheet and two alternative sequences. Avoid photographing identifiable people or sensitive locations.

04 · Critique and correct

Write six accurate captions with observations separated from interpretation. Invite a viewer to infer the sequence, record their words fairly, and compare with your intended story. Revise at least two captions or one frame order to reduce misleading implications. Preserve the original draft.

05 · Reflect and review

Explain what the six frames support, what they do not prove, and how editorial ordering affects narrative. Address consent, privacy, attribution, accessibility and staged scenes. Request independent educator review; formative practice is not certified mastery.

Independent studio packet

Submit a six-frame original visual sequence or drawn alternative, a contact sheet, two alternate orders, six evidence-conscious captions, two attributed documentary examples, a viewer interpretation record, before-and-after revisions, and an ethical reflection. Use a fictional or fully disclosed staged scene; no public upload is required.

Formative practice · Five randomized questions

Five questions drawn from fifteen. Four correct reaches the 80% practice target. Submitted answers lock; a retake begins a new attempt. Practice is not certified mastery.

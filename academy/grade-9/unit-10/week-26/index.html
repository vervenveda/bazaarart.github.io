Week 26 · Photography, Framing and Visual Evidence

Unit 10 · Printmaking and Reproducible Images · Original studio instruction

Essential question

How do framing, viewpoint, light and selection influence what a photograph can legitimately show—and what it cannot establish?

Art 101 is a proficiency-based course for teens and adults. A phone camera is optional: use a drawn viewfinder, paper frames and observation sketches if no camera is available. Work only in permitted spaces and avoid photographing people without appropriate consent. Local evidence is not an authenticated gradebook.

Core vocabulary and reasoning

Frame and field of view

A frame includes some information and excludes other information. Field of view describes the visible extent of a scene. Changing the frame may alter a viewer's interpretation without altering the scene itself.

Viewpoint and perspective

Camera position, height and angle affect relative size, occlusion and spatial relationships. A low viewpoint may emphasize height; an overhead viewpoint may emphasize layout.

Evidence and context

A photograph records a selected view at a moment, not the complete circumstances. Captions, date, location where safe, sequence, editing disclosure and independent corroboration can help distinguish observation from inference.

Original worked demonstration · One table, three frames

Arrange a notebook, cup and pencil on a table. Make three photographs or drawings from the same position: a wide frame showing all objects and surroundings; a medium frame showing the notebook and cup; and a close frame showing only the pencil tip and a page. Write one statement supported by each frame and one statement the frame cannot establish. Keep lighting consistent before comparing.

Worked reasoning: The close frame can support a claim about the pencil's visible shape, but it cannot prove whether a cup was present outside the crop. A wide frame may show more context but still cannot establish what happened before the exposure. Distinguish a visible fact from a hypothesis in every caption.

Five-stage studio lesson

01 · Observe and document

Study three examples of framing in a photograph, painting or illustration. Identify what is included, excluded, emphasized and partially hidden. Note where a crop could change an interpretation.

02 · Research and attribute

Compare two attributed documentary or artistic photographs. Record photographer, title or description, date when known, source and any documented context. Separate what is visibly depicted from what the accompanying source claims.

03 · Experiment and construct

Create three views of one safe still life: wide, medium and close. Hold the arrangement constant, then change viewpoint height for two additional studies. Record camera or drawing method, approximate distance, lighting and framing. Preserve unedited originals if photographs are used.

04 · Critique and revise

Write a caption for each image, marking observations separately from interpretations. Ask another viewer what they can infer and what remains unknown. Revise one misleading crop or caption, keeping before-and-after evidence and disclosing any edits.

05 · Reflect and evaluate

Explain how framing influences narrative and evidence, identify what cannot be concluded from the images, cite sources, and discuss consent, privacy and accessible descriptions. Request educator review separately from the formative quiz.

Independent studio packet

Submit three framing studies, two viewpoint-height studies, a contact sheet or annotated drawing grid, two attributed photographic references, five observation-versus-inference captions, one documented crop or caption revision and a reflection. Use only your own still-life objects and permitted spaces; avoid identifiable people, private addresses and location metadata in shared evidence.

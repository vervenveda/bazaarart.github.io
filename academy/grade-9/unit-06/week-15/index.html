Week 15 · Multiple Viewpoints and Architectural Form

Unit 06 · Space, Perspective and Designed Environments

Essential question

How do artists and designers translate a three-dimensional building into different two-dimensional views, and how does the chosen viewpoint shape what the viewer understands?

Extend Week 14's one-point construction into two-point perspective, architectural plan and elevation, rotation, eye level and visual critique.

Three ideas to master

Two vanishing directions

When viewing a building corner obliquely, two sets of horizontal edges recede toward different points on the horizon. In ordinary level-view two-point perspective, vertical edges stay vertical.

Plan, elevation and perspective

A plan describes layout from above; an elevation presents a face without perspective convergence; a perspective view depicts apparent depth from a particular position.

Viewpoint changes meaning

High, low, near and distant viewpoints alter apparent scale, overlap and which planes are visible. A good drawing records the observer's position rather than inventing a single universal view.

Original worked demonstration · A corner gallery

Step 1: Draw a horizon across the page. Place two vanishing points near the left and right margins (or beyond the paper using an extended ruler guide). Step 2: Draw a vertical front corner of a simple gallery building between the points. Step 3: From its top and bottom, draw light orthogonals toward both vanishing points. Step 4: Mark one rear vertical corner on each receding face. Step 5: Connect each rear corner toward the opposite vanishing point to close the roof or base where visible. Step 6: Place doors and windows using the same receding-edge logic, and keep their vertical sides upright. Step 7: Compare a small plan and elevation to the perspective, then revise one convergence and one scale relationship.

Keep vanishing points far enough apart to avoid exaggerated convergence. A tilted camera or upward gaze may call for a three-point model instead.

Five-stage studio sequence

1 · Observe an architectural corner

Study a building corner, furniture box, or safely observed street view. Mark eye level and sketch visible edges without rulers first. Distinguish verticals from the two sets of receding horizontals. Compare a frontal view with an oblique view, and record what actually changes.

2 · Construct two-point perspective

Draw a horizon with left and right vanishing points widely separated. Place a vertical front corner between them. Connect its top and bottom to both points. Select the side-wall widths and raise two vertical back corners; connect these to the opposite vanishing points to close the top and bottom planes. Lightly retain construction lines for review.

3 · Rotate form and change eye level

Draw the same box from three oblique viewpoints, adjusting which face is more visible. Repeat with a higher eye level to expose more top surface. Draw an architectural plan and front elevation alongside the perspective to compare what each view communicates. Note that vertical convergence belongs to three-point setups, not the usual level-view two-point model.

4 · Design an original corner building

Create a small library, gallery, kiosk or community studio seen from a corner. Include two visible facades, openings, a nearby human-scale object, a clear ground plane and a consistent light direction. Use Week 12 tonal values and Week 13 overlap/scale principles. Save the first draft and correct at least two spatial relationships.

5 · Research, critique and evidence

Compare two attributed architectural artworks or drawings from different eras or traditions. Record creator, title, date if known, medium, institution/source URL and access date. Distinguish constructed perspective from other intentional spatial systems. Ask a reviewer which edges align convincingly, and archive a 120–200 word reflection with revisions.

Studio checklist

Preserve construction lines in at least one sketch. Label horizon, vanishing points, ground contact and the viewer's position.

ORIGINAL VISUAL STUDY · WEEK 06

Three ways colors relate

Use the same simple scene to compare color harmony and contrast.

Look closely at each example, then recreate the comparison with your own materials. The diagrams are simplified teaching models, not substitutes for observation of real light and pigments.

Essential question

How can relationships between colors direct attention, organize space, and influence the feeling of an artwork?

Learning goals: distinguish analogous, complementary, and split-complementary color schemes; compare additive screen blending with physical paint mixing; design a controlled palette; justify an original composition through evidence.

This laboratory evolves the Academy's original preschool Rainbow Mixing Laboratory concept—selection, mixing, experimentation, and challenges—into a secondary-level design investigation. It does not copy the preschool game's child-facing interface or assume digital RGB blending behaves like pigments.

Three color relationships

Analogous

Hues adjacent on a traditional color wheel, such as blue, blue-green and green. Often useful for visual continuity. Vary value and saturation to maintain legibility.

Complementary

Hues across from one another on a chosen wheel, such as blue and orange in a traditional artist's model. Contrast can emphasize a focal point, but results depend on proportion and context.

Split-complementary

One base hue and the two hues on either side of its complement. This offers contrast with more options than a simple two-hue pairing.

Worked example · Design a focal point with complementary hues

Choose a predominantly blue composition with a smaller orange focal point. Make a first thumbnail with equal amounts of each color, then a second with roughly three parts blue to one part orange. Compare where the eye travels. Repeat with a lighter blue and darker orange to test whether value contrast, not hue alone, creates emphasis.

Independent studio experiment · Three thumbnails

Make three small original compositions of the same subject: analogous, complementary, and split-complementary. Label the chosen hues and record one value adjustment and one saturation adjustment for each. Compare focal point, continuity, and readability; revise the strongest composition and archive both versions.

Illustrated reference · Six color relationships

This expanded studio reference adds primary, square, triadic and tetradic arrangements to the analogous and complementary schemes already studied. Use the bright color wheels to identify selected hues, then compare their proportions and values in the examples. The poster is a visual study guide; examples of artworks and historical artists should be researched independently rather than treated as verified reproductions.

Lesson extension · Four additional harmony structures

Primary: In a traditional red-yellow-blue artist model, compare the three primary hue families. Primary colors depend on the model: screen light uses red, green and blue, while printing commonly uses cyan, magenta, yellow and black.

Triadic: Select three approximately evenly spaced hue families on the chosen wheel. Use one as dominant, another as supporting and the third sparingly for emphasis.

Square: Choose four hue families approximately one-quarter of a turn apart. Control complexity by assigning a dominant color and balancing the remaining hues through value and saturation.

Tetradic (rectangle): Choose two complementary pairs. Unlike a square, the intervals need not be equal. Compare warm and cool dominance, then test whether the palette remains legible in grayscale.

Both square and rectangle are four-hue tetradic arrangements in many teaching systems. Wheel geometry is an approximation; do not assume every digital or pigment color space is perceptually uniform.

Guided demonstration · Build and revise a four-color composition
  1. Sketch a simple still life or landscape using only shapes and values.
  2. Select a tetradic palette on the wheel and record the four hue families.
  3. Assign approximately 60% of the color area to a dominant hue, 25% to a support hue, and 15% to accents combined. These proportions are an experiment, not a fixed design law.
  4. Make a second version with different light-dark relationships while keeping the same four hue families.
  5. Compare the focal point and readability. Explain which version communicates your intent better and why.
Research, critique and portfolio evidence

Find two documented artworks from different artists or cultural contexts. Record artist, title, date, medium, collection or reliable source, and access date. Describe the visible hue relationships without assuming an artist deliberately used a modern wheel scheme. Create three thumbnails, select one for revision, and archive the first attempt, revised work, palette notes, source citations and a short self-critique in the Week 06 portfolio.

Week 06 studio assignment

  1. Select a still life, landscape, architectural detail, or original abstract composition. Make a small value-only thumbnail first.
  2. Use the laboratory to plan three alternative palettes: analogous, complementary, and split-complementary. Record hue, saturation, lightness, and your rationale.
  3. Choose one palette and create an original finished composition. Preserve value structure, deliberately control the dominant and accent colors, and explain the intended focal point.
  4. If materials allow, test two physical paint mixtures and compare their observed behavior with the screen blend. Otherwise, compare labeled color swatches or colored pencil layering and document the limitation.
  5. Archive the thumbnail, three palette studies, final artwork, and reflection in the portfolio begun in Week 01.

Use physical art supplies or an offline drawing tool. The laboratory supports planning; a generated palette alone is not the completed weekly artwork.

KHAEMENES ACADEMY · ART 101 · UNIT 11

Week 30 · Layout, Grids and Visual Systems

Unit 11 · Printmaking and Reproducible Images · Original studio instruction

Essential question

How can a grid organize information consistently without forcing every message into the same visual pattern?

Art 101 is proficiency-based for teen and adult learners. Pencil, ruler and paper are sufficient; no design software or font downloads are required. Local evidence is not an authenticated gradebook.

Core vocabulary and visual reasoning

Grid, columns and gutters

A grid is a repeatable alignment system. Columns divide usable space; gutters separate columns; margins provide space at the edges. The grid is a guide to relationships, not a requirement that every element occupy an equal rectangle.

Hierarchy, alignment and proximity

Scale, contrast, placement and whitespace signal importance. Consistent edges and spacing help readers find relationships. Nearby items may appear grouped; intentional separation can clarify different subjects.

Visual system and responsive design

A visual system repeats a limited set of type sizes, spacing rules, colors and component patterns. At narrow widths, columns may stack while reading order, hierarchy and accessibility remain intact. Test at multiple page sizes rather than assuming a desktop arrangement will fit.

Original worked demonstration · Three-column exhibition guide

Plan a printed exhibition guide 210 mm wide. Set left and right margins to 15 mm, with two gutters of 6 mm. The usable content width is 210 − 30 = 180 mm. Three equal columns therefore measure (180 − 12) ÷ 3 = 56 mm each. Draw the grid, then place a large exhibition heading across all columns, a featured work across two columns, and three smaller entries below. On a narrow paper mockup, stack the entries in a meaningful reading order.

Worked comparison: Make two alternative layouts using the same six information items. Record the first three things a reader notices in each. If a featured image takes two columns, explain which content becomes less prominent. A reader test reveals responses under those conditions, not a universal design law.

Five-stage studio lesson

01 · Observe and annotate

Compare three printed or digital layouts: a magazine spread, an exhibition guide and an information card. Trace their alignments, margins, columns, gutters and whitespace. Identify where the grid is deliberately broken and whether that helps the message.

02 · Research and attribute

Investigate two attributed examples of editorial or graphic design. Record creator or institution, work description, date where known, publication or context, and traceable source. Distinguish documented design choices from your own interpretation.

03 · Construct a visual system

Create a two-page exhibition guide for a fictional collection of your own artwork. Specify a grid, at least three text hierarchy levels, a consistent spacing scale and a limited color system. Make a wide version and a narrow stacked version, preserving all required content and logical reading order.

04 · Test and revise

Ask two readers to locate the exhibition title, a work description and the date or location in both versions. Record where they hesitate. Revise a weak alignment, cramped gutter, confusing grouping or inaccessible contrast. Keep the before-and-after designs and describe the change.

05 · Defend and reflect

Explain how the grid supports coherence without suppressing emphasis. Provide the 210 mm width calculation or your own measured equivalent. Discuss narrow-layout reading order, accessibility, attribution and limitations of your test. Request educator review separate from the formative quiz.

Independent studio packet

Submit three annotated layout analyses, a measured three-column grid calculation, two attributed design references, a two-page original exhibition guide, a narrow stacked adaptation, a reader-test record, a documented revision and a visual-system specification. Pencil-and-paper mockups are fully acceptable.

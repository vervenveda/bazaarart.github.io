Week 29 · Letterform and Readability

Unit 11 · Printmaking and Reproducible Images · Original studio instruction

Essential question

How do letterforms, spacing, contrast and reading conditions influence whether a typographic message is understood accurately and accessibly?

Art 101 welcomes teen and adult learners by proficiency. All exercises can be completed with pencil, ruler and paper; no software or font downloads are required. Saved evidence is browser-local, not authenticated mastery.

Core vocabulary and visual reasoning

Letterform anatomy

A baseline aligns the bottoms of many letters. An x-height describes the height of the lowercase x in a typeface. Ascenders rise above the x-height; descenders extend below the baseline. Counters are enclosed or partly enclosed spaces within letters. Observe how these structures affect recognition.

Spacing and rhythm

Kerning adjusts space between a specific pair of characters; tracking adjusts spacing across a range; leading is the vertical distance between lines of type. Excessively tight or loose spacing can interrupt reading rhythm. Compare letter, word and line spacing independently.

Legibility, readability and access

Legibility concerns distinguishing individual characters; readability concerns sustained understanding of text. Contrast, size, line length, spacing and viewing conditions all matter. Do not assume a decorative typeface is suitable for long passages.

Original worked demonstration · Type anatomy and spacing

Draw two headings containing the same phrase, “READ THE ROOM.” In Study A, crowd the letters and use weak contrast. In Study B, provide clear letter spacing, larger x-height in supporting text and strong contrast. Compare at normal reading distance and from across the room. Record which letters become ambiguous and why.

Worked measurement: Draw a text block 120 mm wide. Test line lengths of approximately 20, 35 and 50 characters, recording actual line breaks and reader response. Compare under the same lighting and at the same viewing distance. A small classroom test describes these readers' responses; it does not establish a universal optimum.

Five-stage studio lesson

01 · Observe and annotate

Compare three examples of type from a book, sign and package. Sketch their letterforms and annotate baseline, x-height, ascenders, descenders, counters and distinguishing strokes. Explain which features support or hinder recognition.

02 · Research and attribute

Investigate two attributed typographic works or type-design traditions. Record designer or issuing institution when known, title or description, date where available, source and original use. Distinguish documented design intent from your interpretation; do not guess authorship.

03 · Experiment and construct

Create three original versions of a short informational notice: one decorative, one neutral and one accessibility-focused. Change letterform, spacing, hierarchy and contrast deliberately. Keep the wording identical. Build a hand-lettered alphabet sample of at least twelve characters with marked baseline and x-height.

04 · Test and revise

Test each notice at arm's length and a greater distance, or simulate reduced-size reading. Ask two readers to identify the heading and one detail without prompting. Record errors and conditions; revise the least successful version. Keep both drafts and state what changed.

05 · Defend and reflect

Explain the relationship between type anatomy, reading conditions and accessibility. Support your design decisions with observed results and two attributed sources. Acknowledge limitations of a small test and request educator review independent of formative practice.

Independent studio packet

Submit three typographic analyses, a twelve-character letterform specimen with anatomy guides, three original notices containing identical text, a controlled readability test with two readers or a documented solo simulation, a before-and-after revision, two attributed references and a written defense. Avoid claiming that a small test establishes universal accessibility.

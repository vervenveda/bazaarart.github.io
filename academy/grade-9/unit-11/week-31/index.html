Week 31 · Information Design and Ethical Claims

Unit 11 · Printmaking and Reproducible Images · Original studio instruction

Essential question

How can a visual explanation help people understand evidence without hiding uncertainty, exaggerating differences, or implying conclusions that the source does not support?

Art 101 is proficiency-based for teen and adult learners. Pencil-and-paper information graphics are welcome. Use fictional classroom data for practice, or accurately attributed public data. Never invent real-world statistics or present illustrative figures as verified facts. Browser-local evidence is not authenticated mastery.

Core vocabulary and visual reasoning

Data, variables and encoding

A variable is a characteristic that can take different values. An encoding uses visual position, length, size, color or symbol to represent values. Choose an encoding that matches the question; a precise bar length is often easier to compare than decorative pictograms of unequal area.

Scale, baseline and denominator

An axis scale connects measured quantities to visual distances. A bar chart usually needs a zero baseline to avoid exaggerating differences. A denominator describes the total behind a fraction or rate; changing it can alter interpretation even if a headline number looks similar.

Source, uncertainty and ethical claim

A responsible graphic states its source, units, date or scope, and meaningful limitations. Correlation does not by itself prove causation. A visual claim should be no stronger than the evidence and should disclose selection, missing data and uncertainty when relevant.

Original worked demonstration · Two ways to show the same classroom count

Use the following fictional practice dataset: in a classroom exercise, 12 students selected drawing, 18 selected photography and 24 selected sculpture as a first-choice studio. Total = 12 + 18 + 24 = 54. Fractions are 12/54 ≈ 22.2%, 18/54 ≈ 33.3% and 24/54 ≈ 44.4%; rounded percentages may total 99.9%. Compare a bar chart starting at zero with a misleading chart whose axis begins at 10. Both depict the same values, but the truncated baseline visually exaggerates the difference between 12 and 24.

Worked ethical analysis: The values are counts of preferences in one fictional exercise, not claims about art students generally. Neither chart shows why a choice was made. The honest chart displays the full zero-to-approximately-28 range; the truncated chart should be rejected or explicitly marked as a narrow-range comparison rather than used to dramatize the difference.

Five-stage studio lesson

01 · Inspect a visual claim

Analyze three information graphics. Identify their variables, units, sources, time periods, denominators and implied conclusions. Look for cropped axes, inconsistent symbol areas, omitted categories, inaccessible color choices and unsupported causal claims.

02 · Research and attribute

Choose two attributed examples of public information design. Record issuing institution or creator, title, publication date when known, underlying dataset or source, and a traceable reference. Distinguish the graphic's stated claim from your interpretation and identify missing methodological details.

03 · Design and calculate

Using the fictional 12/18/24 classroom dataset or a clearly documented alternative, calculate totals and percentages. Create a labeled bar chart with a zero baseline and a second representation using a different encoding. Specify scale, units, denominator, source note and any rounding. Use shapes or text labels so color is not the only cue.

04 · Test and revise

Ask two readers to identify the largest category, the actual numerical difference between the largest and smallest categories, and one limitation of the data. Record responses and revise confusing labels, distorted scales or overstated captions. Preserve both versions and explain each correction.

05 · Defend and reflect

Write a claim-evidence-limitation statement explaining what the visualization supports and cannot support. Describe why correlation is not causation, how the denominator affects percentage claims, and why accessible labels matter. Request educator review separately from the formative quiz.

Independent studio packet

Submit three annotated graphic critiques, two attributed design references, a calculation sheet, two original representations of one documented dataset, a caption with source and limitations, a two-reader interpretation test, before-and-after revisions and an ethical visual-claim defense. The classroom dataset is fictional; label it accordingly.

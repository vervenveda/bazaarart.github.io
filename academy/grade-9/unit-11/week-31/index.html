KHAEMENES ACADEMY · ART 101 · UNIT 11 · WEEK 31

Information Design and Ethical Claims

Typography, Graphic Design and Information · Original visual inquiry and studio instruction

Essential question

How can a visual explanation represent measured quantities, sources and uncertainty without distorting what is known?

This course supports teenagers and adults and includes equipment-free alternatives. Studio evidence and quiz scores are stored in this browser only; they are not authenticated grades, identity or automatic course credit.

Core vocabulary and reasoning

Infographic

A visual arrangement of information combining text and graphical representations.

Scale

The relationship between a visual measure and the data value it represents.

Baseline

A defined reference value, such as zero in many bar charts.

Denominator

The total number or population to which a fraction or rate refers.

Uncertainty

A stated limit in precision, confidence or completeness of available evidence.

Source label

A citation that allows a viewer to trace where a figure or claim came from.

Pictogram

A repeated symbolic image used to represent a quantity or classification.

Original worked example

An art-club infographic reports 18 out of 30 survey respondents prefer morning workshops. A careless icon chart shows 18 icons beside only 10 for afternoon, even though twelve respondents preferred other times.

  1. Verify that the total sample is 30 and write a source line: 'Fictional classroom example: 18 of 30 respondents, 60%'. Avoid implying a whole city or school was surveyed.
  2. Draw a bar from a clear zero baseline with values 18 and 12; label the denominator and categories. For a pictogram, make every icon equal one respondent and show thirty icons in total.
  3. Create a misleading comparison by cropping the bar baseline, then correct it and explain the difference in perceived effect without implying a real finding.
  4. Present the graph together with a plain-language summary, sample size, method limitations and a question inviting viewers to request the underlying data.

Five-stage studio investigation

01 · Observe and document

Observe: Analyze two public charts, separating the plotted quantities from rhetoric. Check axes, labels, visual units, percentages and whether important categories are omitted.

02 · Research and attribute

Research: Locate one sourced public dataset or use an explicitly fictional class sample. Record original publication, sample definition, collection date and usage rights. Do not invent precision.

03 · Experiment and construct

Experiment: Represent the same numbers as a labeled bar chart, a pictogram and a short plain-text table. Compare how quickly each format communicates totals and uncertainty.

04 · Create, critique and revise

Create and revise: Build a one-page infographic for an art supply inventory or simulated community survey. Run a reader test asking which group is largest, what the denominator is and how uncertain the claim is.

05 · Reflect, archive and seek review

Reflect and archive: Keep the raw numbers, chart drafts, source ledger, revision notes and a final statement separating directly supported claims from speculation.

Independent studio assignment

Design a source-labeled infographic with at least two graphical forms, a data table, clear denominator, true zero scale where appropriate, a limitations note and an audience comprehension test with documented revisions.

No-special-equipment alternative

Use graph paper, repeated paper-square pictograms and handwritten labels. Use an invented dataset clearly labeled 'synthetic classroom example'.

Privacy, safety and rights

Avoid unsupported statistics and manipulative area scaling. Cite all actual data and provide numerical text alongside graphics for accessibility.

Research and attribution

Explore these institutional starting points. Choose specific attributed works yourself and record creator, title, date if known, accession or catalog page, rights details, and what remains uncertain.

  • Smithsonian · Accessible resources. Verify creator, title, date, page and reuse rights for any specific artwork selected.
  • Library of Congress · Digital resources. Verify creator, title, date, page and reuse rights for any specific artwork selected.

Collections and archives contain materials with varied rights. Public accessibility does not automatically establish permission to republish.

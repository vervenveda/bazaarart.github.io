# Bazaar Art 101 — Week 01: Lines & Shapes
**Status:** instructional enrichment candidate; not yet connected to the live six-week portal.
**Audience:** beginners of all ages. **Materials:** paper and pencil; optional eraser, charcoal, markers, or recycled cardboard. No purchases required.

## Learning goals
By the end, learners can (1) distinguish contour, gesture, and construction lines; (2) identify geometric and organic shapes; (3) build recognizable objects from simple shapes; (4) compare proportion and negative space; and (5) explain one revision with evidence from observation.

## Lesson 1 — A line is a decision
A line can describe an edge, suggest movement, or organize a drawing before its final contours appear. A **contour** follows the visible boundary of a form; a **gesture line** captures direction and energy; a **construction line** helps position and proportion shapes and may later be erased. Lines can vary in weight, length, direction, and rhythm. These variations guide attention, but a darker line is not necessarily more accurate.

**Demonstration:** Place a cup on a table. Draw its outside edge slowly without shading. Then draw the same cup using faint construction lines: a central vertical axis, an ellipse for the rim, and two tapering sides. Compare which method made its symmetry easier to judge.

**Practice:** Make a page of straight, curved, broken, and continuous lines. Draw three 30-second gestures of a safe stationary object (such as a plant) and one five-minute contour study. Label the types of lines used.

## Lesson 2 — Shapes are the building blocks
**Geometric shapes** such as circles, rectangles, and triangles have regular mathematical properties; **organic shapes** are irregular and often resemble forms found in nature. Artists simplify complex subjects into large shapes before adding details. **Positive space** is the area occupied by the subject; **negative space** is the area around and between subjects. Neither is automatically more important.

**Demonstration:** Draw an apple as a circle or oval, then revise its silhouette with an indentation at the stem and an irregular bottom. Place a leaf beside it and inspect the empty wedge between the two shapes. Ask whether the space helps clarify their positions.

**Practice:** Select three household objects. For each, draw a simple geometric breakdown, then an observed outline. Include one drawing of the negative space between two objects.

## Lesson 3 — Proportion, placement, and revision
**Proportion** describes size relationships among parts; **composition** is the arrangement of visual elements. A shape can be recognizable yet look unstable if its parts are incorrectly positioned. Use a pencil as a measuring guide at arm's length, keeping your viewing position consistent. Compare relative widths and heights, not absolute centimeters. Revision is evidence of learning, not failure.

**Demonstration:** Draw a mug as a tall rectangle with an ellipse and a curved handle. Check whether the handle attaches at plausible points and whether the ellipse matches your viewing angle. Revise after comparing with the real mug.

**Practice:** Create a “Shape Zoo” of three to five objects or animals using construction shapes. Choose one drawing and create a second version with improved proportions or placement.

## Studio assignment and evidence
Submit or retain in a private sketchbook: (a) line studies; (b) three shape breakdowns; (c) one negative-space study; (d) Shape Zoo with at least three subjects; (e) one before/after revision; (f) an artist reflection explaining which change improved clarity. Learners who cannot draw by hand may use accessible mark-making tools, collage, or an assistive device while demonstrating the same concepts.

**Young learner option:** Explain the reflection aloud to a guardian/teacher, who may transcribe it. **Advanced extension:** Compare two arrangements of the same objects and justify how negative space changes the visual hierarchy.

## Formative check — 10 questions
1. What is a contour line? **Answer:** A line tracing a visible boundary.
2. What does a gesture line emphasize? **Answer:** Movement or overall direction.
3. Why are construction lines often light? **Answer:** They guide placement and can be revised.
4. Name one geometric shape. **Answer:** E.g., triangle, circle, rectangle.
5. What distinguishes organic shapes? **Answer:** Irregular, nonuniform contours.
6. What is negative space? **Answer:** The area around/between subjects.
7. What is proportion? **Answer:** Relative sizes of parts.
8. Does a darker line always mean greater accuracy? **Answer:** No.
9. Why draw large shapes before details? **Answer:** To establish structure and placement.
10. What should a useful revision statement identify? **Answer:** A specific change and its observed effect.

## Mastery rubric (educator-reviewed)
Four criteria, each scored 0–4: line control and purpose; shape analysis; proportion/negative space; reflection and evidence of revision. A score of at least 13/16 (81.25%) meets the Academy's >=80% threshold. Students below mastery receive targeted feedback and may revise without losing earlier work. Avoid scoring artistic taste, style, speed, or access to expensive materials.
